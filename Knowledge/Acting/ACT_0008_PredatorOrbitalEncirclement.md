# Definition

Predator Orbital Encirclement is a physical blocking technique in which a dominant character takes slow, deliberate, silent steps in a partial or full arc behind an adversary who is actively speaking, positioning themselves progressively deeper into the adversary's rear proxemic zone. No words are spoken by the encircling character. The physical violation of personal space — particularly the rear, which the adversary cannot visually monitor — triggers involuntary physiological breakdown in the target.

# Purpose

This skill solves the problem of how to make a speaking character reveal guilt, fear, or dishonesty without confronting them directly. By silently occupying the space behind them, the dominant character converts their own body into a pressure instrument — extracting truth through spatial violation rather than interrogation.

# Psychology

The rear proxemic zone (behind the shoulder-plane) is the most psychologically vulnerable spatial region in human threat response. Unlike frontal threats — which the nervous system can assess and respond to — a presence behind the back cannot be visually verified, triggering sustained low-level threat processing that the conscious mind cannot shut down. A speaking person attempting to maintain a composed verbal narrative while experiencing rear-zone violation will begin losing motor precision: the executive-function resources required to sustain fabricated speech are overwhelmed by the simultaneous demand to process an unverifiable threat. This causes the specific cascade observed: laryngeal elevation (swallow), ocular saccades, lip tremor, vocal pitch rise, stammering. The body dissolves the lie because it cannot do two things at once.

# Story Function

The technique converts a testimony, interrogation, or adversarial speech scene into a visible performance of guilt. The audience watches the adversary's narrative physically disintegrate in real time without any direct accusation. This is more dramatically satisfying than a verbal confrontation because the truth is extracted by the scene's physical architecture rather than stated by any character.

# Character Function

The encircling character communicates complete awareness of the adversary's deception, supreme physical confidence, and absolute moral authority — all without a single word. The silence itself is the most powerful statement they could make: it signals that they do not need to argue, because the truth will surface on its own under sufficient spatial pressure.

# Internal State

The encircling character is calm, focused, and certain. They have already reached their conclusion before entering the orbit — the encirclement is not investigation; it is demonstration. There should be no performance of anger, impatience, or suspicion. The colder and more composed the encircling character remains, the more terrifying the technique becomes.

# External Behaviour

Four to six slow, even steps in a 90°–180° arc behind the adversary. Zero upper-body bounce — the torso must remain completely stable. Stride pace approximately 0.3–0.5 m/s — slower than a normal walk. Arms remain at the sides or in a natural relaxed position. Gaze directed at the adversary's neck or the back of their head — not breaking to look at the audience or other characters. The encircling character should position themselves within 0.4–0.5m of the adversary's back at the closest point.

# Dialogue Guidance

The encircling character speaks no dialogue during the orbital movement. If dialogue is required before or after the encirclement, it should bracket the movement: a short statement before the first step, silence during the orbit, a single short statement or question after completing the arc.

# Voice Guidance

Voice is not relevant during the orbital movement itself. Any statement preceding the orbit should be delivered in a calm, quiet register — the contrast between spoken calm and silent physical action amplifies the technique. See VOI_0001 (Low-Volume Vocal Threat) for the vocal framing before and after the orbit.

# Body Language

Every element of the body must communicate ease and inevitability. The encircling character should not look like they are performing an intimidation technique — they should look like someone quietly examining a situation they already fully understand. Hands relaxed, jaw loose, breathing even. The adversary's distress is the performance; the encircling character's composure is the instrument.

# Timing

Begin the first step while the adversary is mid-sentence — never between their sentences, which would allow the adversary to acknowledge or react to the movement. The orbit should complete (or reach maximum proximity) at approximately two-thirds of the adversary's speech duration, leaving the final third for the adversary to speak while the encircling character is fully positioned behind them. The technique achieves maximum pressure at the moment of full rear positioning.

# Rhythm

Slow, deliberate, and completely even. Each step should take approximately 1.5–2.0 seconds. There should be no variation in pace — accelerating suggests impatience; decelerating suggests hesitation. Evenness signals absolute control.

# Camera Considerations

A slow 180° orbital tracking shot following the encircling character mirrors their physical movement and puts the audience in the encircling character's perspective. This is distinct from a static two-shot: the orbital camera movement makes the audience co-conspirators in the spatial trap. Shallow depth of field isolating the two figures removes the surrounding court or room, collapsing the world to this single spatial relationship.

# Lighting Considerations

If the encircling character passes behind a directional light source, their shadow falling across the adversary's back provides a visual metaphor for the technique's psychological mechanism: the shadow arrives before the body does. This can be designed intentionally in scenes where the lighting rig allows.

# Music & Sound Considerations

Silence or near-silence is most effective during the orbital movement. A low drone or subtle pulse score may be used, but it must not exceed the volume or presence of the adversary's stammering speech — the deterioration of the adversary's voice is itself the most important sound element in the scene.

# Best Used When

- An adversary is delivering a fabricated or dishonest testimony.
- The dominant character has already privately reached a conclusion about the adversary's guilt.
- The scene requires truth extraction without direct accusation.
- The adversary's physical or psychological vulnerability needs to be made visible to the audience.
- The setting allows for uninterrupted physical movement (an open floor, no obstacles behind the adversary).

# Avoid When

- The adversary is aware of the technique and would directly address or physically respond to the encirclement.
- The scene requires a verbal confrontation where the dominant character's argument, not their body, must win.
- The adversary's speech contains information the audience genuinely needs to hear clearly — the technique's pressure may disrupt intelligibility.
- The setting is physically constrained (tight quarters, crowds) where a 180° arc is impossible without drawing attention.

# Common Mistakes

- Moving too quickly: any pace faster than 0.5 m/s reads as aggression rather than predatory calm.
- Upper-body bounce during the steps: breaks the impression of absolute physical control.
- Making eye contact with other characters or the camera during the orbit.
- Starting the orbit too early — before the adversary has committed to their narrative and begun to relax.
- Stopping short of full rear positioning: the technique requires the encircling character to reach the adversary's back, not merely their side.
